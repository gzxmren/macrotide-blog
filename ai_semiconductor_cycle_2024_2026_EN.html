From Supply Anxiety to the Return Reckoning: Making Sense of the 2024–2026 AI & Semiconductor Bull Market

The engine of this rally was never end-market demand in the traditional sense. It was a small handful of companies placing an early bet on AI’s future cash flows. To understand it, you have to track hyperscaler capital expenditure — and how the market’s attitude toward that spending has shifted over time.

July 22, 2026 · Written for readers fluent in both finance and technology · Not investment advice

1. This Is Not a Traditional Semiconductor Cycle

Semiconductors are a classically cyclical industry. For decades, their ups and downs were driven by a familiar set of forces: the replacement cadence of end devices (phones, PCs), enterprise IT budgets, and the build-and-drain of inventory. The industry even has a tested playbook for spotting a top — softening consumer demand, bloated channel inventory, falling spot prices.

The 2024–2026 cycle is different. Its demand does not come from a diffuse end market; it is highly concentrated in the AI infrastructure investment of a few hyperscalers. In other words, this cycle’s “demand” is, in essence, the capital-spending decisions of four or five companies acting on the same thesis. The old inventory-and-consumption framework is largely useless here. A more useful lens is to track these companies’ appetite for capital expenditure (capex), and how the market’s view of that spending evolves.

This piece follows that thread. It breaks the rally into three phases, explains why it moved from the “supply anxiety” of 2024 to the “return reckoning” of 2026, and then discusses the new questions now on the table and the paths ahead.

2. Phase One: Supply Anxiety (2024 to early 2025)

The starting point was less about demand than about a belief layered onto a game of competition.

The belief came from scaling laws — the empirical observation that, over a meaningful range, model capability improves steadily with more parameters, more data, and more compute. Once the industry broadly accepted this, the conclusion was direct: whoever invests more, and faster, in compute is more likely to lead on the next generation of models.

The competitive game came from structure. For each cloud provider, the worst outcome is not “we spent too much” — it is “we fell behind because we underspent.” When rivals are expanding compute, no one can afford to sit still. Capex, in this phase, was read by the market as a kind of option premium: a defined amount of money paid to buy a call on “not missing the AI race.” With the upside seen as open-ended and the outlays still manageable relative to company size, the market rewarded spending almost unconditionally.

On the supply side, that demand was met along two paths: merchant GPUs, led by Nvidia, and custom ASICs co-developed by the cloud providers with the likes of Broadcom and Marvell (the in-house accelerators — TPU, Trainium, Maia, and so on). Either way, the “pick-and-shovel” semiconductor names were the most direct beneficiaries. The hallmark of this phase was a clean demand narrative with little disagreement: share prices and spending expectations rose together.

3. Phase Two: The Reflexivity of Capex (2025)

Through 2025, the rally took on a distinctly reflexive character.

The mechanism ran roughly like this: rising share prices lowered the cost of capital and emboldened management to keep spending; higher spending translated into stronger revenue and guidance for the semiconductor suppliers; that in turn lifted expectations for the whole chain, which pushed prices higher again. Each earnings season, “raising the capex guide” became almost an expected move, and the market rewarded it — semiconductors rose in step.

Crucially, in this phase cloud names and semiconductors rose together; there was no trade-off between them. Capex was still seen as a purely positive signal — “commitment” for the one spending, “orders” for the one collecting. Disagreement had yet to surface, but risk was quietly accumulating: demand was growing ever more concentrated in a few buyers, and those buyers’ spending pace depended increasingly on cooperative sentiment and financing conditions.

4. The Turn: From “Are You Spending Enough?” to “When Does It Pay Off?” (2026)

In 2026 the market’s question turned on a pivot: from “are you spending enough?” to “when, and in what form, does this money come back?”

This was not a mood swing. Several financial facts crossed their thresholds at roughly the same time:

  • Capex is now growing much faster than revenue. The year-over-year growth in capital spending has clearly outrun cloud revenue growth, and “return on investment” is being quantified and questioned for the first time.
  • Capex has, for the first time, exceeded operating cash flow across the board. On disclosed figures, the major players’ combined FY2026 capex guidance approaches the $700 billion range — roughly double the prior year — and each company’s spending now exceeds its own entire operating cash flow. That is a watershed: these firms are no longer “fighting this war out of profits,” but starting to fight it off the balance sheet.
  • The financing mix is changing. When internal cash no longer covers the spend, financing shifts from operating cash toward debt — and even to off-balance-sheet special purpose vehicles (SPVs). Once leverage enters the picture, the market’s pricing logic switches from “pricing growth” to “pricing return on capital and solvency.”
  • The depreciation wave is coming ashore. The capex laid down in 2023 and 2024 is now flowing into the income statement as depreciation and amortization (D&A), compressing gross margins. Some companies have already guided to double-digit-billion increases in annual depreciation; others’ margins have fallen to multi-year lows. Capex is a cash outflow today; depreciation is a non-cash expense tomorrow — the depreciation wave is precisely the bridge that carries already-strained free cash flow onto the P&L.
  • Time is passing, and the endgame has not arrived. Neither AGI nor any widely acknowledged step-change in capability has shown up within the expected window. As the ultimate payoff keeps receding, the “when does it pay off?” question only sharpens.

Stacked together, these facts produced a repricing of the very same capital expenditure: in the market’s eyes, it went from a “call option” (open-ended upside, worth buying) to a “fixed cost” (a defined drag, with an uncertain return). The same dollar of spending — the frame changed, and so did its sign. This repricing is central to understanding the price action of 2026.

    • Microsoft is supported by multiple quarters of Azure acceleration and the ready cash flow of enterprise software. But its gross margin has slipped to a multi-year low, so the question this week is whether the drag from spending and depreciation on margins is starting to show.
    • Amazon has a relatively solid demand side, but its free cash flow has been sharply compressed by enormous spending. It runs the largest absolute capex of the four — and is therefore the most exposed on free cash flow. The read is whether AWS growth holds and how far free cash flow deteriorates.
    • Meta’s position is changing. It has had no public cloud to monetize externally; its AI spending has served its own ads and products. But it has now explicitly signaled a move into compute rental / public cloud, an attempt to open an external monetization path for its vast infrastructure. There is, however, no related order or revenue data this quarter — and in a “show me the receipts” environment, an announced-but-unproven path reads to the market as an option rather than a cash flow. It remains the one story most in need of validation.
  • Depreciation assumptions and earnings quality. Each company smooths earnings by adjusting the useful life of servers and networking gear: Amazon has shortened its estimate in recent years (more conservative, recognizing expense sooner), Meta has lengthened its (flattering current earnings), with Alphabet and Microsoft in between. Watch this week for any company that revises its depreciation assumption again — that is often a signal about earnings quality.

Put the four together and the week is really testing four sides of the same question: spending is still accelerating, but each company draws on a different source of “ballast” to support that spending, and the market will price them accordingly. Which sets up the core tension discussed below — staying power.

5. A Structural Lens: The “Seesaw” Between Cloud and Semiconductors

The previous section points to a phenomenon peculiar to the current phase: at certain moments, cloud names and semiconductors move inversely, like a seesaw.

The logic is simple. The same capital expenditure is a cost line on a cloud company’s statements and a revenue line on a chipmaker’s — opposite signs by nature. When the market is focused on “is this money worth it?”, a single earnings report can be bearish for the spender and bullish for the seller at the same time.

But three boundaries must be placed on this observation, or it is easily mistaken for a stable trading rule:

  • The base state is positive correlation, not inverse. On most trading days, cloud and semis are components of the same “AI trade,” driven by the same risk appetite; they rise and fall together. The seesaw appears only briefly, in earnings windows, when the market re-prices “spending efficiency.” It is a second-derivative phenomenon.
  • It holds only in one specific quadrant. Only the combination of “capex raised × monetization weak” produces cloud-down, semis-up. If capex rises while monetization delivers in tandem (revenue accelerating, margins holding), the two rise together — that is not a seesaw.
  • It belongs only to this phase. More precisely, the seesaw is a product of one specific window: where investment far exceeds return and AGI has not yet arrived. The market can read opposite signs into the same dollar precisely because the return is unproven and the ultimate verdict is not yet in. Once monetization matures (return catches up with investment) or a step-change arrives (spending is retroactively justified), the window closes, and the two return to being priced together on fundamentals.

The seesaw also carries an asymmetry: semiconductors are, in essence, the “arms dealer” of this war. Whether the buyers’ bets ultimately win does not directly affect the arms dealer’s current receipts — but that holds only up to the point where the buyer can still afford the next order. So semiconductor fortunes are downstream of the cloud providers’ staying power. As long as capex holds, the arms dealer keeps benefiting; but the moment a buyer cuts spending first, it is the arms dealer that gets hit first.

6. Internal Divergence: Semiconductors Are Not a Monolith

Treating “semiconductors” as a single bloc obscures a divergence that matters more and more in this cycle. The direction of capex largely determines who within the chain benefits and who comes under pressure:

  • Merchant GPUs versus custom ASICs. Cloud providers are buying commercial GPUs while also accelerating their own accelerators. The push into custom silicon means that even as total capex keeps growing, the share and pricing power of merchant GPU suppliers can be eroded — “the whole pie grows” and “one player falls behind” can coexist.
  • Memory and HBM. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) is one of the key bottlenecks in AI training, and its cycle has partly decoupled from that of commodity consumer memory. That means calling a top in memory can no longer rely on the traditional “consumer demand weakening” signal; you have to watch the inflection in contract-price growth and the product mix (the HBM share).
  • Foundry and equipment. Leading-edge foundry is the true bottleneck of the whole chain, and its capex guidance is often a leading signal of demand; raising foundry capex usually means end demand is real. Semiconductor equipment sits further upstream, driven by the pace of capacity expansion.

For this cycle, there is a “seesaw within semiconductors” — different segments have different sensitivities and directions to the same dollar of capex. Going long or short “semiconductors” as a bloc misses this structure.

7. The Core Question: A War of Attrition Before the Payoff

Pulling the threads together, the core question of the current phase reduces to one line: can each party endure before the return arrives?

This is a war of attrition, not a contest of who spends the most. What decides it is staying power:

  • Who has a ready cash cow to fund the campaign. Companies with strong existing businesses (search, enterprise software) can support spending out of profits and endure the longest; those relying mainly on debt or equity dilution, without matching cash flow, come under pressure first. This explains an apparent contradiction: faced with the same high capex, the market treats different companies very differently — the difference between being “waved through” and being “punished” often lies not in the spending itself but in the staying-power rating.
  • It is a prisoner’s dilemma. Each company may well understand that collective over-investment will ultimately erode overall returns; but as long as rivals keep spending, none dares to stop first. Which means capex will not self-correct until someone blinks. And the day someone “blinks first” — a company visibly cutting spending — is likely to be read as a demand top, at which point semiconductors take the first hit and the shock spreads to the broader set of AI-linked assets.

Applied to this week’s four reporters, the differences in staying power are clear:

  • Alphabet and Microsoft sit at the high end. With mature, high-margin cash cows — search for one, enterprise software for the other — they can fund AI investment out of profits for a long time. The market’s relative tolerance of their elevated capex is, in essence, an endorsement of that staying power.
  • Amazon is in the middle. AWS and retail cash flow provide support, but its absolute capex is the largest and its free-cash-flow compression the most visible — its staying power depends on whether AWS growth holds and whether the pace of spending stays controllable.
  • Meta faces the most pressure on this dimension. Lacking a public cloud to monetize externally, its AI investment has leaned on its own ad profits and outside financing rather than an independent cash flow matched to the spend. Its announced move into compute rental / public cloud is precisely an attempt to convert a “pure cost center” into such a cash flow — but that path has no order revenue this quarter, and entering a market already dominated by AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud as a latecomer carries real execution risk. Until it is proven out, the market will likely treat it as “unproven.”

It bears emphasizing that a high staying-power rating does not mean these stocks are free of downside — it measures “how long one can hold out,” not “whether one will be repriced.” Even the strongest of the four, if its cash-cow business itself softens or its margins are crushed by the depreciation wave, would send the market’s judgment about whether the whole complex “can endure to the good days” materially weaker. That is exactly why the first card of the week matters: it tests not just one company’s quarter, but whether “the one with the most ballast is also starting to strain.”

The market’s anxiety, then, is fundamentally about two things: who runs out of endurance first, and whether the “good days” (mature monetization or a capability breakthrough) arrive before anyone does.

8. Outlook: Several Possible Paths

The future is better discussed as scenarios than as forecasts. The paths below are neither mutually exclusive nor assigned any prior probability:

  • Monetization gradually catches up (a soft landing). Cloud revenue and margins keep improving and progressively underwrite the spending; the market returns to pricing on fundamentals. The cycle extends but shifts from “narrative-driven” to “cash-flow-driven,” with volatility compressing. The relatively benign case.
  • A step-change arrives (low probability, high uncertainty). If a widely acknowledged breakthrough in capability appears, the prior wave of enormous spending would be retroactively “justified,” and the whole complex could re-rate upward together. The timing and shape of this path are extremely hard to predict, and it should not be treated as a base case.
  • A crack in the war of attrition (a tail risk). A company cuts spending first, triggering a reassessment of demand; semiconductors come under pressure first and the shock spreads to the wider set of AI assets. A downside scenario to be taken seriously.
  • High-level plateau, chronic digestion (muddle-through). Capex stabilizes at a high level, depreciation grinds on margins, and the sector churns and diverges — with single-name differentiation, rather than beta, becoming the main story.

Whichever path prevails, the variables worth tracking are broadly the same: the gap between capex and operating cash flow; the scissors between spending growth and revenue growth; the real bite of the depreciation wave on margins; the weight of debt in the financing mix; the pace at which custom silicon substitutes for merchant GPU share; and — most important of all — when the first “blink” comes, and from whom.

9. Conclusion

From the outset, the driving force of the 2024–2026 semiconductor bull market was never end-market demand in the traditional sense. It was an early bet, by a few companies, on AI’s future cash flows. The market’s attitude toward that bet has moved from “supply anxiety” to “the reflexivity of capex” to “the return reckoning”; and the same capital expenditure has been repriced from a coveted call option into a fixed cost to be scrutinized.

At this juncture, the question is no longer “how much are they spending,” but “can they endure until the return arrives — and what will that return actually look like.”

This supports neither unconditional optimism nor a one-sided bearish case. It is more like an open question about endurance and delivery — one that will be answered, quarter by quarter, through capex guidance, monetization receipts, and margin performance.
