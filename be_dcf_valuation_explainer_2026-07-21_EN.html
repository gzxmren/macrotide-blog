Finance basics · a real case

A $197 stock that's "worth" $9

A plain-English guide to how stock valuation actually works — using Bloom Energy (BE) as a live example, and unpacking the two ideas inside DCF that trip most people up: the discount rate, and reverse-engineering the price.

As of 2026-07-21 · Educational walkthrough of a valuation method · Not investment advice

Here's a fact that makes people do a double-take.

In July 2026, fuel-cell company Bloom Energy (ticker: BE) trades at $197 a share — a market cap of about $56 billion. But when you sit down and estimate what it's worth based on the cash it will actually generate, the number that comes out is roughly $9 a share.

A 20x gap. That's not a math error — it's two concepts most retail investors have never had explained cleanly. Let's use BE to walk through both.

1. What are we even calculating?

The classic way to value a stock is called DCF (Discounted Cash Flow). The name sounds intimidating; the idea is dead simple:

A company is worth however much real cash it will hand back to owners in the future. So we do three things: ① project how much spendable cash it earns each year for the next 10–15 years; ② translate that "future cash" into what it's worth today (future money is worth less than money in hand — this step is the whole game); ③ add it up, divide by the number of shares, and you get "what one share should be worth."

Compare that to the market price, and you know whether it's cheap or expensive right now.

2. Running it forward: every version lands far below the price

I used a set of already-generous assumptions (BE grows fast for a decade and steadily gets more profitable), then changed exactly one thing — the "discount rate" — to get four versions of the value:

Across all four numbers, I never touched a single assumption about the business (growth, profitability — identical every time). The only thing that moved is one dial: the discount rate. That one dial swung the value from $52 down to $9 — nearly a 6x difference. Understand it, and you've grasped the heart of valuation.

3. What is the "discount rate"? And why is it applied every year, compounding?

Picture someone handing you an IOU: "I'll pay you $100 ten years from now." What's that worth today? Definitely not $100 — because you have to wait ten years, and it might never pay out (what if the company goes under?). So today you'd only pay something less than $100. How much less is the discount rate. The less you trust it and the riskier it is, the steeper the discount.

Is it a per-year rate? Yes — and it compounds, stacking year on year. Think of it as interest running in reverse: if $100 today grows at 12% a year, it becomes $310 in ten years; so, flipped around, "$310 ten years out" is worth only $100 today. Discounting is that process played backwards.

The same $100, discounted at 12% a year, is worth this much today depending on when you get it:

When you get itMathWorth today
1 year out100 ÷ 1.12$89
2 years out100 ÷ 1.12²$80
3 years out100 ÷ 1.12³$71
5 years out100 ÷ 1.12⁵$57
10 years out100 ÷ 1.12¹⁰$32

Notice the pattern: it's not "12% total over ten years," it's "12% every year, layered on top of itself." So the further out the cash, the harder it gets hit — $100 ten years away is worth just $32 today.

4. Why does a small change in the discount rate swing value by 6x?

Two things stack into a "double whammy":

First, discounting compounds, so it punishes the far future exponentially. Cash ten years out is worth about 1/9 of face at a 25% rate, but about 1/2.6 at a 10% rate. The gap widens exponentially.

Second, for a growth stock like BE, almost all the value sits many years out. It barely earns anything now; most of its estimated worth rides on a big imagined pile of cash "ten-plus years from now." And that far-future cash is exactly what a high discount rate destroys.

BE's stock swings about 3.74x as hard as the market (that number is called beta, and the market computes it directly). More swing = more risk = a steeper discount rate. A company that "only earns later," meeting "high risk = high discount rate," gets hit from both sides. That's why $9 and $52 can be so far apart.

Going deeper: how does a beta of 3.74 turn into "about 25%"?

We said "more risk = higher discount rate." But how do you turn risk into an actual percentage? Wall Street uses a formula called CAPM, with just three ingredients. Keep one line in mind: the discount rate is really just the annual return investors demand to hold this stock — riskier means they demand more.

▪️ Ingredient 1 — Risk-free rate ≈ 4.3% (the floor): what you earn taking zero risk (a 10-year US Treasury). If even zero-risk pays 4.3%, any stock has to beat that or you'd just buy the bond.
▪️ Ingredient 2 — Equity risk premium ≈ 5.5% (the reward for holding stocks at all): historically, because stocks are volatile and can lose money, investors demand roughly 5.5% a year above Treasuries. This is the reward for owning an average, moves-with-the-market stock.
▪️ Ingredient 3 — Beta = 3.74 (the risk multiplier): how much wilder this stock is than the market. Beta = 1 moves with the market; BE at 3.74 is a beast that moves 3.74% when the market moves 1%.

Stack them — start at the floor, then add the "reward for stock risk," scaled up by how wild this particular stock is:

Discount rate ＝ Risk-free rate ＋ Beta × Equity risk premium
＝ 4.3% ＋ 3.74 × 5.5%
＝ 4.3% ＋ 20.6% ＝ ~25%

In plain terms: an average stock (beta 1) earns a 5.5% risk reward; BE swings 3.74x as hard, so investors demand 3.74x that reward = 20.6%; add the 4.3% floor and you're at ~25%. One line — it's roughly 4x as violent as the market, so investors want nearly 4x the risk compensation of a normal stock, pushing the required return to 25%.

(Technical note: strictly, the rate used to value a company is the WACC — a blend of the cost of equity and cost of debt. But BE has almost no debt, so its WACC ≈ cost of equity ≈ 25%.)

⚠️ This step is exactly where DCF shows its soft underbelly
Of the three ingredients, beta is the least reliable — it's backward-looking, and 3.74 is precisely what BE's recent wild run cooked up. Measure it over a different window and you might get 2.5, which drops the discount rate to ~18% and doubles the valuation. So 25% isn't "the one correct answer" — it's "what the textbook tells you to use, given how insane its volatility is right now." Which proves the point again: for a story stock, DCF can't hand you a trustworthy price target — the uncertainty in a single input can swing the value wildly. Its value is in the direction, not the precision.

5. Running it backward: "is it expensive?" → "what would it have to do?"

Running it forward, people can argue about assumptions forever ("I think it grows faster!" "the discount rate should be lower!"). So there's a sharper move — reverse it.

Instead of asking what it's worth, you assume the $197 price is correct, then work backwards: what would this company actually have to achieve to deserve $197?

I held "grows fast" fixed, used a normal 12% discount rate, and dialed up just one knob — "how profitable it eventually becomes" — until the math finally justified $197. The answer:

This company would have to reach: for every $100 of sales, it keeps $60 in pure cash.

"How much pure cash you keep per $100 of sales" (technically, free cash flow margin) means: sell $100 of product, and after paying to build it, pay salaries, build factories, pay taxes, and buy equipment, how much spendable cash is left. Measured against real companies:

Company typeCash kept per $100 of sales
A hardware maker like BE (fuel cells / steel / components / installation)realistically $10–15
Apple (premium hardware)~$25
Microsoft, Visa (most profitable software/payments on earth)~$30–40
What $197 requires of BE$60
That $60 is essentially unprecedented
It asks a company that sells steel boxes of energy hardware to be twice as profitable as Microsoft. Businesses that make physical equipment have basically never hit a 60% free-cash-flow margin. So $197 is betting on a level of profitability with almost no real-world precedent.

Can "insane growth" rescue it instead?

I tried the other path: skip the margin, lean on growth — assume revenue 13x's over a decade (to $27 billion) with a respectable $15-per-$100 margin. The implied price is still only $78 — 60% below $197. Translation: growth alone can't save it; you'd need both insane growth and an impossible margin to reach $197.

Why reverse-DCF is so useful
It converts a squishy argument about assumptions into a single, checkable claim: "paying $197 means betting it hits a 60% cash margin." Then you just ask yourself one thing — can a company that builds fuel cells be twice as profitable as Microsoft? That's a far easier question than arguing over discount rates, and the bubble pops on contact.

6. The bottom line: are you buying a story, or cash flow?

Put both ends together: run it forward, BE is worth $9–$52; run it backward, $197 demands an impossible level of profitability. Four versions, two directions, all saying the same thing: on the math of cash, the price is far out of reach.

So where does $197 come from? A very seductive story — "AI data centers are starved for power, the grid can't keep up, and Bloom's fuel cells can generate electricity on-site." The story might be true, and the stock might keep climbing on it. But be clear-eyed: at this point you're betting on narrative and momentum, not discounted cash flow.

This is not a call that "it will crash"
Momentum stocks can detach from fundamentals for a long time, and if the story delivers, they can run even higher. This method also won't hand you a "buy price." What it really does is act as a ruler — measuring just how wild the expectations baked into today's price are. Read that ruler, and at least you know what you're betting on.

The whole thing in three lines:

① The discount rate shrinks future cash "every year, compounding" — the further out and the riskier, the harder the hit.
② Forward asks "what is it worth"; reverse asks "what would it have to do to be worth this" — and the second is much harder to hand-wave.
③ When no reasonable calculation gets near the price, you're mostly buying a story, not cash flow. Know that, then decide whether to make the bet.
