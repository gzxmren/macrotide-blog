Google just posted a "perfect" quarter — and the stock fell. This is the real turning point for the AI capex story.

GOOGL Q2 2026 deep-read · lightweight DCF · read-through to NVDA / AVGO / MRVL / Oracle / SMH · not investment advice

Alphabet delivered a near-flawless quarter: revenue +24%, Cloud +82%, EPS a blistering $9.11 (+294%). Then the stock fell after hours.

The market isn't being dumb. This is the first shot in AI capex re-rating from "is demand there?" to "are the returns there?" Here's the breakdown — and what it means for the whole chip complex.

1｜That EPS is a mirage: $9.11 isn't real

  • Net income +298%, EPS +294% looks explosive. But "other income" jumped from $2.7B to $98.0B.
  • Google's own footnote: mostly UNREALIZED gains on equity holdings — a mark-to-market paper number. Non-cash, doesn't touch free cash flow, and it can flip to a huge paper loss the moment markets dip.
  • Strip it out and "clean" EPS is roughly $3. Anyone shouting "earnings blowout" off $9.11 is reading it backwards. Judge Google's real profitability on operating income +30%, nothing else.

2｜What the market actually feared: free cash flow went negative + a rare equity raise

  • This quarter capex was $44.9B — for the first time exceeding operating cash flow of $39.1B → quarterly free cash flow of −$5.9B.
  • In June, this famously cash-hoarding, buyback-machine company issued $49.6B of equity (including mandatory convertible preferred) and set up a $40B at-the-market program — explicitly to fund AI infrastructure.
  • A money-printing cash cow now diluting shareholders to pay for capex only happens when the spend has outgrown what the business itself can fund.
  • And guidance was raised again: FY26 capex from $180–190B to $195–205B, with 2027 signaled higher still.

3｜A lightweight DCF: what's it actually worth?

  • Finding 1: the negative FCF itself barely dents value — discounted, it's worth about −$6/share. The selloff is more sentiment than valuation.
  • Finding 2: what really pressures value is whether capex permanently resets higher as a share of revenue. Move terminal capex from 15% → 24% of revenue and per-share value drops ~35%. That's the entire bet: is AI compute a temporary buildout or a permanent new normal?
  • Three scenarios: bear $98 / base $184 / bull $311 vs a ~$342 price. All three sit below the current price — to justify $342 you need the bull case AND a low discount rate.
  • The gut-punch counterfactual: assume Google never entered the AI arms race (capex back to a normal 15%, slower growth) → DCF says $183. The base case burning $200B → $184. Nearly identical. Meaning: in the base case this whole war is roughly NPV-neutral — running flat out just to stand still — with all the value riding on the bull tail actually landing.

In one line: capex didn't crush the base-case value, but it stretched the downside tail enormously. $342 is a conviction price — "the capex WILL pay off" — with no cash-flow margin of safety.

4｜The seesaw: Google's pain is the chipmakers' fuel

Here's the key to the whole picture — every dollar Google spends is someone else's revenue. GOOGL gets punished for burning cash, but that cash is real orders for chip and infra vendors. So read the semis inversely to Google's stock price.

5｜Read-through, name by name: who wins, and how much

Broadcom (AVGO) — the most direct winner

  • Google's TPU is essentially a Broadcom co-designed custom chip. TPU v8 ramping + "Virgo linking a million accelerators across data centers" + capex to $205B feed Broadcom's two AI engines directly: custom XPUs + networking silicon (inter-datacenter interconnect).
  • Google is the most custom-silicon-committed hyperscaler → when it raises capex, Broadcom has the highest leverage to it.

NVIDIA (NVDA) — rising tide, but at Google its share is diluted by TPUs

  • Higher total AI capex + "supply constrained" is bullish for total GPU demand and pricing power.
  • But the catch: Google is precisely the most TPU-heavy cloud, so its own dollars skew toward Broadcom TPUs, not NVIDIA GPUs. Net positive on the bigger pie, but Google is where NVDA gains the least share.
  • NVDA is the sector's floor and bellwether — wait for its own print to confirm; don't extrapolate NVDA share straight from Google's capex.

Marvell (MRVL) — second-tier winner; optical interconnect is the sweet spot

  • Custom ASIC + optical/interconnect DSP. "Virgo connecting across multiple data-center sites" = long-reach optical demand = a tailwind for Marvell's optical DSPs.
  • But its custom-silicon share at Google is smaller than Broadcom's — call it second in line.

TSMC (TSM) — the picks-and-shovels of the picks-and-shovels; steadiest

  • Every one of these chips (NVDA GPUs / Broadcom TPUs / Marvell / Google's Axion CPU) is fabbed at TSMC with CoWoS packaging. Industry-wide capex up = wafer and advanced-packaging demand up across the board — the most winner-agnostic, lowest-volatility read.

Oracle (ORCL) — double-edged, and most exposed under the new lens

  • Bull side: Google's $514B backlog + "supply constrained" validates the entire demand backdrop — good for Oracle's own giant backlog story.
  • Bear side (the important one): Oracle is the poster child for a debt-funded, deeply FCF-negative capacity buildout. If the market now looks at AI clouds through a "capital discipline / free cash flow" lens — and Google just fired that shot — Oracle is the most exposed: it's funded by debt not equity, runs lower margins, and is a near-pure NVIDIA GPU buyer (no custom-silicon cost lever). Highest beta to this sentiment shift.

SMH (semis ETF) — demand tailwind, but watch the seesaw

  • Weighted toward NVDA / TSMC / Broadcom / AMD, so the demand tide lifts it.
  • The risk: if the market rotates from a "demand" narrative to a "capex discipline / FCF" one, cloud multiple compression can spill over. Google's stock vs SMH the next day is the live test — SMH up while GOOGL down = seesaw confirmed; both down = "capex scares the whole chain" risk-off is winning.

6｜What really matters for semis isn't this earnings season — it's the day FY27 numbers "crystallize"

There's an easily-missed information structure here: Google gave a clear FY26 number ($195–205B), but for FY27 only said "significantly higher" — no number.

  • No number = everyone fills in their own, and in a momentum tape they fill it high (the market currently pencils Google's FY27 around ~$250–290B). That penciled-in figure is the invisible "imagination premium" embedded in semis' valuations right now.
  • Here's the key: when the real FY27 number lands, what moves semis isn't how big the number is — it's the gap between the number and what the market already imagined. Semis can get a huge number and still fall, as long as it's short of what was penciled in. (That's the mirror of what just happened to Google itself: capex up = growth, stock down.)
  • And because momentum sets the bar high, the closer you get to the number, the more asymmetric the payoff: limited upside, open downside.

When does it crystallize? MSFT + META (Jul 29) and AMZN (Jul 30) give a first calibration via their 2027 language; the real binary catalyst is the Q4 earnings season in late January 2027, when each hyperscaler hands over a formal FY27 capex number and imagination becomes a measured expectation.

In one line: semis are currently held up by an "imagination premium" — any hawkish-capex data point can push it higher; the closer we get to the numbers crystallizing, the more the odds tilt down.

7｜The week ahead: the bar has been raised

  • Google beat on everything and still fell — that sets the tone: demand without near-term returns gets punished anyway.
  • MSFT + META (Jul 29) and AMZN (Jul 30) now get measured with the same FCF ruler. META is the most fragile — it has no cloud to monetize the capex (it was already hit −10% last quarter for spending alone).

Bottom line

Demand is no longer the question — Google proved it to the extreme. The new question is returns, free cash flow, and who funds it. With one flawless quarter and one ugly cash-flow line, Google turned the page for the entire AI complex.

For the chip chain: it's fuel (the more they burn, the more orders flow). For cloud stocks: it's a stress test (who can make the spend make sense). Broadcom eats most directly, Oracle has the most to prove, and NVIDIA hinges on its own print.
